## 1. The High-Precision Evaporation Scale  

    What's being measured: The exact weight of the water in a basin

    Why it's being measured: To calculate the evaporation rate

    How it's being measured: The basin sits on a load cell (a highly sensitive digital scale). A temperature sensor and a small wind sensor (anemometer) sit above the basin to track the air conditions causing the evaporation

    Expected readings: A slow, continuous drop in weight (e.g., losing 3 grams per hour), with the rate spiking if you turn on a fan or a heater nearby

    Significance of readings: It proves the direct mathematical link between local weather (heat/wind) and water loss

    What to look out for: Physical vibrations. Walking heavily past the table or bumping the desk will cause massive spikes on the scale

    Real-world impact: High evaporation rates lead to rapid drought onset and crop failure


## 2. Acoustic Precipitation Monitor  

    What's being measured: The physical impact force, size, and frequency of individual falling water droplets

    Why it's being measured: To classify the type of water event (e.g., a slow condensation drip vs. heavy simulated rain) 

    How it's being measured: Water drops hit a rigid, drum-like surface containing a piezoelectric disc (a sensor that turns physical vibration into electricity)

    Expected readings: Sudden, distinct spikes in voltage. A small drip from a sponge might read as a 0.5V spike, while a heavy drop from a height reads as a 3.0V spike

    Significance of readings: It maps the kinetic energy of a weather event. Ten small drops and one massive drop might contain the same amount of water, but they interact with the environment differently 

    What to look out for: Acoustic interference. If the sensor is too sensitive, a door slamming, loud music, or a heavy truck driving by outside could trigger a false "rainfall" reading 

    Real-world tactile impact: Large, heavy raindrops carry energy, which violently strips away topsoil and causes sudden flash flooding. This is a critical issue in monsoon regions or deforested areas like parts of Southeast Asia. Acoustic sensor data is used to trigger early-warning mudslide alarms or automatically raise physical flood barriers before the actual water volume has time to accumulate and destroy infrastructure 


## 3. Custom Optical Turbidity Chamber

    What's being measured: Water cloudiness, caused by particles like dirt, sediment, or algae

    Why it's being measured: To determine water purity and monitor contamination levels 

    How it's being measured: A laser or bright LED shines through a sealed light-proof tube submerged in the wate. If the water is clear, the laser shoots straight past. If the water is dirty, the particles scatter the laser light sideways into the sensor.

    Expected readings: Very low light readings in clean tap water. When you stir dirt, sand, or a drop of milk into the basin, the light readings will immediately spike as particles scatter the beam

    Significance of readings: It provides a quantifiable number for how "dirty" the water is, directly reflecting the density of physical pollutants

    What to look out for: If sunlight or gets into the testing chamber, it will ruin the laser readings

    Real-world tactile impact: High turbidity blocks sunlight from penetrating rivers and oceans, killing underwater plants and suffocating fish by clogging their gills with sediment
